📖 How to Play

Human AI Prompt Lab is a game about teaching your own AI. Play in a team of 3–4 and see how smart you can make it!

1
Teach your AI
On the left panel, pick a knowledge type, write a question or trigger, and write a clear answer. Click Teach AI to add it to your AI's memory.
2
Chat with your AI
On the right panel, type a question the way a real person would. Your AI can only answer using what you've taught it — nothing more.
3
Watch it grow
Every memory grows your AI's brain. Reach new levels — Baby AI, Learning AI, Smart AI, Expert AI, Super AI — as you teach more.
4
Complete missions & earn badges
Open the Missions and Badges panels (bottom corners) to see challenges and achievements you can unlock.
Knowledge Types
Fact — objective information, e.g. "What is a cat?" → "A cat is a mammal."
Rule — a response for a situation, e.g. "Hello" → "Hello! Nice to meet you."
Identity — who the AI is, e.g. "What is your name?" → "My name is Robo."
Personality — how the AI behaves, e.g. "Are you friendly?" → "Yes! I always answer politely."
Good to know If your AI says "I haven't learned that yet," that means you need to teach it! A good AI needs clear, specific answers — vague answers make it confused.

FOR TEACHERS ONLY

🧑‍🏫 Teacher Hub

This section is for the facilitator, not students. It explains the lesson goals, a suggested class flow, the concepts behind the game, and how to talk students through it.

Learning Objectives
  • Students explain, in their own words, that an AI only "knows" what it has been given — it does not think or understand on its own.
  • Students practice writing clear, specific, unambiguous instructions (an early form of prompt engineering).
  • Students recognize the difference between this simplified training game and a real AI model like ChatGPT.
  • Students collaborate in a team to plan, divide, and test knowledge entries.
  • Students connect the activity to broader ideas in digital citizenship and computational thinking: data quality, categorization, and testing/debugging.
Preparation
  • Group size: 3–4 students per device/team.
  • Suggested duration: 45–60 minutes (a trimmed 30-minute version is included at the end of the Lesson Flow tab).
  • No installation or internet needed — everything runs and stays inside the browser tab. Refreshing resets the AI.
  • Open the "How to Play" guide once for the whole class before letting students start (it explains Knowledge Types, Missions and Badges from the student side).
  • One device per team (laptop/tablet), any modern browser, no login required.
  • Optional but recommended: a projector or shared screen to run one live demo before teams start on their own.
  • Optional: pre-assign each team a different "expert topic" (animals, science, history, or your own subject) so teams end up with different AIs to compare later.
  • A visible clock, timer, or countdown on the board helps teams pace themselves through each phase.
  • Review the Concepts and Script tabs yourself before class so you can explain the "why" behind the game, not just the "how".
Differentiation & Extension
  • Teams that finish early: challenge them to reach Expert AI or Super AI level, complete every mission including Mission 7 (device swap), or teach one "trick" fact with a very specific trigger phrase for another team to discover.
  • Teams that need more support: give them 3–5 ready-made fact ideas on paper (about your subject or the school) to translate into Question/Answer pairs, and pair their Tester with a stronger team once their own teach phase is done.
  • For an older or more advanced class: skip the training wheels and ask teams to design their AI as a "customer service bot" or "class FAQ bot" for a specific real scenario, then discuss where exact-match logic would fail in production.
Detailed Class Agenda (60 min)
Before class
Setup
Get devices ready, decide team groupings, and (optionally) assign each team a different expert topic so their AIs turn out different.
  • Open this Teacher Hub once yourself and skim the Concepts and Script tabs.
  • Decide teams of 3–4 and write the team list / expert topics on the board.
  • If using a projector, open a second copy of the app to run the live demo.
0–5 min
Hook
Ask: "Does AI actually think, or is it doing something else?" Take a few guesses, don't correct them yet.
  • Cold-call 2–3 students for their guess; write key words on the board without judging right/wrong.
  • Tell them: "By the end of class, you'll have built your own tiny AI and can answer this yourselves."
Tip: keep this fast — the goal is curiosity, not an answer yet.
5–15 min
Mini-lesson & live demo
Run the five Knowledge Lab screens first: what a prompt is, C–T–C–O, vague vs clear prompts, test-and-improve, and the difference between this game and generative AI. Then teach one memory live so students see the activity loop once.
  • Open "Learn First" and walk through the C–T–C–O framework. Ask students to identify Context, Task, Constraints, and Output in the example prompt.
  • Live demo: teach one Identity memory (e.g. "What is your name?" → its name), then chat and get a correct answer.
  • Live demo continued: ask the same question a different way (e.g. "Who are you?") and show the class it fails — this previews the key lesson without spoiling the discussion.
  • Briefly point out the Missions (🎯) and Badges (🏅) buttons as their checklist for the next phase.
Tip: let the class react out loud when the demo AI "fails" — that surprise is what makes the later debrief land.
15–20 min
Team setup & roles
Get teams settled, name their AI, and split up simple roles so all 3–4 students are involved.
  • Trainer: types the Question/Answer into the app.
  • Checker: reads each answer out loud before it's submitted — is it clear and specific?
  • Tester: will be in charge of the chat/testing phase later.
  • Have every team set their AI's name in the header field before moving on.
Tip: rotate roles halfway through the teach phase so everyone gets to type at least once.
20–38 min
Teach phase (main activity)
Teams work through the Missions panel in order. Circulate constantly and check that answers are specific, not vague.
  • Recommended order: Identity memory first (Mission 2), then a Rule/greeting (Mission 3), then 5 Facts (Mission 1), then their expert topic (Mission 5 or 6).
  • At the ~29 minute mark, do a 1-minute "brain check": ask teams to call out their memory count and Brain Level out loud for friendly competition.
  • Watch for vague answers ("it's nice", "some stuff") — stop and ask "would a stranger understand this answer?"
  • Watch for duplicate/contradicting entries from different teammates typing the same trigger with different answers.
Tip: this is the longest phase — don't rush it, most of the learning happens here.
38–46 min
Test & try to break it
Testers chat with their own AI, then (Mission 7) swap devices with another team and try to stump each other's AI by asking the same question a different way.
  • First, each team quizzes their own AI using the quick-hint buttons and their own questions.
  • Then swap devices with a neighboring team for Mission 7 — encourage rewording, not just retyping the same question.
  • Ask teams to note down one question their AI got wrong and why, ready for the debrief.
Tip: the "unknown answer" bubble (dashed orange) is a teaching moment, not a failure — point that out if a team gets discouraged.
46–54 min
Present & wrap-up
Each team clicks "End Session" and briefly presents their AI's name, level, and favorite memory it learned.
  • Give each team 30–45 seconds max — name, Brain Level, one memory they're proud of, one question that stumped it.
  • Use the Scoreboard numbers (Knowledge Stored, Correct Answers, Completion %) as a quick shared reference while presenting.
Tip: celebrate the team whose AI got stumped in an interesting way just as much as the "smartest" AI — that's the richer learning moment.
54–60 min
Debrief & real-world connection
Use the Discussion tab questions to connect the game back to how real AI is trained, and close the loop on the opening question.
  • Revisit the opening question ("Does AI actually think?") and ask students to answer it now, in their own words.
  • Use the Script tab's closing line to introduce how real models like ChatGPT differ in scale and understanding.
Trimmed 30-Minute Version
  • Hook + Mini-lesson combined: 5 min (skip the live demo, just show the guide).
  • Team setup: 2 min (skip formal roles, just pick who types first).
  • Teach phase: 13 min (only Missions 1–3: facts, identity, rule).
  • Test phase: 5 min (skip the device swap, test their own AI only).
  • Debrief: 5 min (use only Discussion questions 1 and 4).
Concepts to Understand Before You Teach
1. This is not a real chatbot — and that's the point
Under the hood, this AI only checks whether the student's message exactly matches, or contains, a question/trigger they already taught it. It has no understanding of meaning. This is intentionally simple so students can see the mechanism clearly — real models like ChatGPT are trained on huge amounts of text and generalize to new wording, which is why they can answer things they were never explicitly taught.
2. Knowledge Types mirror how AI training data is organized
Fact = objective information. Rule = a fixed response to a situation (like a greeting). Identity = who the AI is. Personality = how it behaves. Sorting knowledge this way is a simplified version of how real datasets are labeled and categorized before a model is trained.
3. "Garbage in, garbage out"
A vague or inconsistent memory makes this tiny AI unreliable. Use that experience to introduce a related prompt-writing habit: clearer Context, Task, Constraints and Output reduce ambiguity when working with generative AI. Do not imply that this Question/Answer memory system is how a large language model is trained.
4. Why paraphrasing "breaks" the AI
If a student teaches "What is your name?" but then asks "Who are you?", the AI will likely say it hasn't learned that — even though a human would understand it's the same question. This is the best moment to explain the gap between simple matching and true language understanding.
Sample Facilitation Script
Opening line"Today, you are not going to use an AI — you are going to build one. It starts out knowing absolutely nothing. Everything it can say by the end of class is because you taught it."
Before the teach phase"Before you type, agree as a team on the answer first. A messy answer creates a confused AI — a clear answer creates a smart one."
Before the test phase"Now try asking your AI the same thing in a different way. Watch what happens — and think about why."
Closing line"Real AI systems like ChatGPT work on the same basic idea — learning from what they're given — just at a massive scale, and with a much deeper understanding of language."
Discussion Questions
  • Why did your AI fail to answer a question it "should" have known, just because it was worded differently?
  • Which Knowledge Type was hardest to write clearly, and why?
  • What would happen if two teammates taught the AI two different answers to the same question?
  • How is a real AI like ChatGPT similar to — and different from — the AI you just built?
  • If you had unlimited time, what would you teach your AI next?
Assessment Ideas (Optional)

This is primarily a formative, engagement-based activity — not a graded test. If you'd like light-touch evidence of learning, the app's own Scoreboard and Badges already give you most of what you need.

Clarity of answers
Are Facts, Rules, Identity and Personality entries specific and understandable to a stranger, or vague ("it's nice", "some stuff")?
Coverage across Knowledge Types
Did the team use all 4 types thoughtfully, or only pile up Facts? Check the Knowledge Library's colored tags.
Successful testing
Use the Scoreboard's Correct Answers and Completion % as a quick, built-in measure of how well-taught the AI turned out.
Collaboration
Did all teammates contribute (Trainer / Checker / Tester roles), and can each student explain one memory they personally added?
Conceptual understanding (exit ticket)
Ask each student to write one sentence: "My AI got a question wrong because ___." A correct answer should reference exact/keyword matching, not "it's dumb" or "it's broken."
Common Issues & Fixes
"My AI never understands me!"Check that the taught Question/Trigger closely matches what they're typing in chat. Encourage teaching a few different phrasings of the same question as separate memories.
Students copy the exact same facts as another teamAssign each team a different expert topic (animals, science, history) so their AIs end up different, then compare during the presentation phase.
A team finishes very earlyPoint them to Mission 7 (swap devices and try to stump another team's AI) or challenge them to reach a higher Brain Level (Expert AI / Super AI).
Progress seems lostEverything lives only in that browser tab. Warn students in advance not to refresh or close the tab before "End Session".
